On a normal working day I have 50 or more agent sessions open on my laptop, and in any given half hour I'm switching between a half-dozen of them. The rest are waiting: on a reviewer, a pipeline, a merge, or an infrastructure change. Over the last week, the local software I run to feed those agents took about 300 actions without me, including 50 times it woke an inactive session to deal with something new and 30 times it completed the final stages of code review and merged.

I've been treating this year as a year of relearning what it means to be a software engineer, and I think I am close to articulating what it is. The engineer still exists and is still necessary, but the job has been retooled around agents to the point where the old description, someone who reads and writes code most of the day, is only true in the vaguest sense. Anyone, any "vibe-coder", can run an agent to build a piece of software. (Some of them even write about it on the internet!) What still separates an engineer from a person with an AI subscription is three things: how much work they can keep in flight at once, how complex a project they can carry to completion, and whether the result meets a quality bar and stays maintainable for a team months and years later.

## Parallelism, or latency hiding

For most of my career, a working day meant pushing along one or two tasks. I'd spend hours reading the code around a change, some time thinking through the design, a day or two writing it, and a large, unpredictable amount of time going back and forth with stakeholders about whether the change should happen at all. A CI run might take twenty minutes and a review might take half a day. Those waits were small next to the work, and I could fill them with more of the same task: writing the tests, reading the next file, scheduling the next meeting. Having several independent tasks moving at once was mostly a way to thrash your deep-work productivity with context switching.

The agentic era compressed the reading, the designing and the writing, and nothing else. The reviewer still takes half a day. The pipeline still takes half an hour. Product manager alignment still takes a week. So a task now spends most of its time waiting on something outside the agent's control, or mine.

This reminds me of a well-worn problem from my computer engineering coursework. A modern CPU core can execute several instructions per clock cycle, but fetching a value from memory takes hundreds of cycles. A core that waited for every load would spend most of its life doing nothing. Cores got much faster while memory latency barely improved, so the industry eventually had to stop trying to win on speed alone. It focused on throughput instead, by keeping more work in flight. Pipelining overlaps the stages of consecutive instructions. Simultaneous multithreading gives one core two instruction streams, so when one stalls, the other runs. GPUs take the idea to its conclusion: rather than trying to avoid stalls, they keep thousands of threads resident and switch to whichever ones are ready. In GPU programming this is called latency hiding. None of these techniques make a single instruction finish sooner, but they make more instructions finish per second.

In the CPU analogy, agentic software engineering has a slow component, and it's us. The AI is the lightning-fast core. Humans are the slow memory, waiting to load a chat register with their thoughts on the matter. So, as with the CPU, the system gets more done if we run more tasks in parallel and point the slow humans at whichever stages are ready for them. Easy, right? Mostly. But other parts of the workflow also have to be rebuilt around fast agents and slow humans. In particular, each task has to spend as little time as possible waiting on a human to do something.

## Automate every click

Amdahl's law, a well-known rule of parallel computing, says that speeding up one part of a process improves the whole only in proportion to that part's share of the time. Suppose coding used to be 80% of a task's elapsed time, and the other 20% was everything around it: clicking merge once a review was approved, moving the ticket, asking a reviewer to take another look, starting a deploy, and all the time the task sat waiting for me to notice it was ready for one of those. Make the coding ten times faster and the task only gets about 3.6 times faster. The clicking, which used to be a fifth of the time, is now more than 70% of it.

Before agents, nobody optimized this, because it was expensive to do and it wasn't a blocker. If I noticed an approval ten minutes late, I lost ten minutes on a task that took three days. Now the same ten minutes lands on a task whose coding took an hour, and my being late is a large share of the whole task.

Almost all of those clicks can now be automated. GitLab, JIRA and Slack have APIs, most tools have an MCP server, and a well-set-up development environment already holds credentials for all of them. So lately a large part of an engineer's job is ensuring that every non-consequential stage transition is also automated.

By consequential I mean a transition that is hard to undo or that needs real judgment: applying an infrastructure change, settling a design question, approving someone else's work. A non-consequential transition is mechanical once its inputs are known: arming a merge once a human has approved and the pipeline is green, moving a ticket after its pull request lands, retrying a job that failed because a CI runner disappeared. Most of the clicks in a working day are the second kind. They only felt like work because a person had to be there to do them.

The obvious first way to automate these is to let each agent do it: tell the session to watch its pull request and act when something changes. This breaks down the way distributed systems usually do. Every session polls GitLab or JIRA separately, each in a slightly different way. There's little central auditability or control, and you're mostly hoping each agent does the right thing. It's also wildly inefficient: 50 agents might all poll the same API endpoints, and each may periodically spend an AI turn interpreting the responses, burning millions of tokens.

Like any good software engineer, I applied the system design principles that we ruthlessly quiz each other on in interviews. I had an agent build an event daemon to poll my data sources (GitLab, JIRA and a few others), work out what changed, and write it to a single ordered event log. In its first two weeks it recorded 9,399 events of 52 types. A separate control plane reads that stream and decides how to treat each event using a routing table of about three dozen rules. Some reactions are purely mechanical, and the control plane handles those itself, calling back to external APIs. When a decision needs an AI's judgment, the control plane messages, or starts, the Claude session that owns the task. The session does the thinking and reports back exactly one verdict. If the session can't handle it cleanly, the system notifies me.

What makes this work well is that facts, control, implementation and hard judgment each live in a place suited to them. The event daemon records the facts. The control plane does the routing. The agent sessions own the implementation and naive decisions, and I hold the final responsibility.

Some things the system deliberately won't do. Nothing in it runs an infrastructure deploy (a Terraform apply): it triggers the plan, a session reviews the plan against the change and posts a summary, and the apply waits for me. When a session handling review comments finds a thread that touches the approach, an API, a schema, authorization or concurrency, it changes nothing, writes up a plan, and reports that it needs me. When a reviewer has been quiet for a day and a half, the system drafts a polite nudge and then asks me whether to send it, because the social cost of pestering a colleague is on me. (Nobody likes getting Slacked by an AI.)

## Why it doesn't run itself

The argument we hear from the business side goes something like this: if developers are using agents for most of their work, why shouldn't the company run the agents centrally and skip (paying) the developers? It's certainly a question. The agents are writing a lot of the code, and most of what I described above is automation. If one person can run 50 sessions, a platform team could presumably run 5,000.

I have an obvious stake in the answer, but I don't think centralization works. About 99% of the tasks I see still need a human to at least eyeball what the agent proposes to do. About half need a major correction to the path of execution. If someone outside engineering specs a small task that happens to require a contentious platform change, or that hardcodes logic that should be generic, the agents will happily try to do it. However good they are at coding, agents are also hopelessly suggestible. They do what the task asks, even when it flies in the face of good engineering practice or the company's interests. They will build infrastructure that doesn't generalize, write meaningless tests, and run up compute costs that make no sense for a startup.

The second reason is predictability. After living with the agents' blind spots every day, I'm not convinced you can tell in advance which tasks will come through clean. Whether a task passes doesn't seem predictable from its content or its inputs. The centralization plan quietly depends on triage: send the easy tasks to the central fleet and the hard ones to people. If nobody can sort them beforehand, every task needs a human nearby, and the human who can catch the problem is the one who knows that domain. And when the agent runs on a remote platform, it's hard to inspect its context and see why it made the decisions it did.

The third reason shows up over time. A centralized fleet works until it doesn't. Agents build at remarkable speed right up until they hit the painful consequences of a bad architectural decision they made early on, and that kind of problem doesn't generally resolve with more agent sessions. The usual suspects are poor concurrency control, missing transactions, misjudging the scale the system will run at, unneeded database indexes, pedantic tests, and expensive evaluators.

The place this has shown up most clearly for me recently is CI. The current models, as of late 2026, love adding tests and CI jobs. From the model's side this makes sense. A test is a deterministic gate on its own output: if it writes a test and some later agent breaks the behavior, the failure is caught. You can see why the frontier labs would reinforce this in training. Tests catch the model's misfires, or at least detect them, which makes the model more reliable in practice than it is on its own.

What the model doesn't do is weigh the cost. It doesn't ask what a job costs in compute, how much time it adds to every pipeline, how often it flakes, or whether the 400th unit test tells anyone anything the 399th didn't. Each addition is locally reasonable, and the sum is a CI system that grows a little with every pull request. 10 minutes, 6 jobs; what's a few more? I've recently found myself spending a growing amount of time pushing back on that: rationalizing CI jobs, cutting their compute, and re-deciding which tests are worth what they cost.

I don't expect this particular problem to last. The next training cycle will probably make the models more cost-conscious. But the pattern will outlast this instance of it. Whatever blind spot the model has becomes an area that software engineers have to manage, and the agents' speed means the blind spot compounds quickly. A centralized fleet without engineers in it just hits the same blind spots at breakneck pace.

## Tooling and trust

So what should a company do instead? I think the answer is to empower engineers rather than route around them: give them the tooling and the trust to build reliable, high-throughput setups, measure what comes out, and gently feed what the organization learns back into each engineer's workflow.

Tooling is the easy part to describe. Engineers need API access to the systems they already use, MCP servers for those systems, self-service credentials that let their agents act, and enough compute and model budget that their day-to-day isn't a procurement exercise.

Trust is harder, and especially hard for the business side. It means letting each engineer or team decide how much to automate and where the consequential line sits for their own work. Mine sits where it does because I've watched where the agents succeed and fail, day in and day out. An engineer on a different codebase sees different conditions, develops different instincts, and should be free to draw the line somewhere else.

Measure outcomes, not activity, which should be obvious: milestones reached against defects and incidents, and revenue against costs.

The last piece is the one an organization is in a unique position to do. One engineer finds a blind spot, like CI cost creeping up one pull request at a time. Ten engineers find it independently and fix it ten different ways. The organization can see across all of them, notice the pattern, and offer the fix back as a shared skill, a review check, or a paragraph of guidance. It should do this gently, because a mandated central workflow would throw away the local judgment that found the problem in the first place. The organization's job is to be the aggregation layer for best practices.

## What the job is now

Software engineers still exist, and are still needed to run software-driven systems, despite how much the agents can do quickly on their own. The job has been retooled around them: work in flight, complexity carried, and quality held over the long term. Most of the day goes to deciding what the agents should work on, removing the mechanical steps between them, and catching their training biases and the things they can't see.

Much of that comes down to judging what is consequential and when to change course. That line moves every few months. Each time it moves, something we used to do by hand becomes automated, and something we hadn't anticipated becomes a problem for the most capable software engineers to manage. I don't know what the next one will be, which is a decent statement of why the job will still exist.
