This has been a year of long outages at the companies the rest of the economy runs on. In January, Microsoft 365 went down across North America for about eleven hours, taking email and Teams with it for most of a working day. In February, a bad cleanup job at Cloudflare knocked a quarter of its bring-your-own-IP customers off the internet for six hours. In late September, an Azure incident during maintenance took network gateways down in eighteen regions for nearly six hours. In November, Microsoft 365 failed again, this time in more than seventy countries, and wasn't fully restored for two days; NHS trusts cancelled operations and went back to paper, and traders went back to the phone. And yesterday, a software bug at JPMorgan Chase slowed customer transactions and backed up Zelle payments at other banks, the third straight weekday with a major technical failure.

My claim is that Big Tech has adopted a management theory that doesn't understand where its long-term value comes from. For a technology company, much of that value is what I'll call engineering capital: the understanding of how its systems actually work, how to change them safely, and how to evolve them to perform better or meet new customer needs. For all the code, the documentation and the new capabilities of AI, most of that understanding still lives in the heads of the people who have spent years working on those systems and the problems they solve. When a company loses that capital, outages and slow recoveries are the first visible symptom. The underlying condition is a company that is losing the ability to manage the delivery of its own technology, and that matters more than any single bad day, because these are innovation businesses. Their valuations are bets on what they'll build next, and what they build next gets built on top of the systems they already run, by the people who understand those systems. An organization that no longer knows its own technology well enough to keep it running doesn't know it well enough to change it boldly either. It can still announce new things. It struggles to ship them, and what it does ship tends to be a feature bolted onto the side of something nobody fully understands anymore.

The management theory that does this has a history. Jack Welch built it at General Electric in the 1980s, and his protégés carried it to Boeing, which spent twenty years converting its engineering capital into short-term profits and executive bonuses. Boeing has since had to pay far more than it saved to buy that capital back. The same accounting now runs at Microsoft, Amazon and Meta, with AI spending in the role that return on assets played at Boeing.

## The Welch school

Jack Welch ran GE from 1981 to 2001, and the most consequential thing he did there was make layoffs normal at a company that was making money. For most of business history, a company cut jobs when it was in trouble, and a layoff was a sign of distress. Large postwar American companies tended to do what the economist William Lazonick calls "retain and reinvest": keep their people and their earnings and put both back into the business. In Welch's first five years, GE's payroll fell from 411,000 to 299,000, and about 81,000 of those jobs were cut from businesses GE kept. The company was profitable throughout. The cuts earned him the nickname Neutron Jack, after the bomb that leaves the buildings standing and removes the people. He treated cutting as routine management, something a well-run company does in a good year, and the rest of corporate America followed. In January 1996 AT&T announced 40,000 job cuts while its profits were strong, and that March the New York Times ran a seven-part series called "The Downsizing of America." Lazonick's name for the new model is "downsize and distribute."

The rest of the kit followed from the same logic. He created the vitality curve, now usually called forced ranking: managers ranked everyone each year, rewarded the top 20 percent, kept the middle 70 and let the bottom 10 go. Outsourcing moved work to whoever could do it cheapest. And managers were held to ratios, the most consequential being return on net assets, or RONA: profit divided by the assets used to earn it. A company can raise its RONA by earning more or by owning less, and owning less is much faster.

By the measure it cared about, the theory worked for twenty years. GE was worth about $600 billion in 2000, the most valuable company in the world. Then GE Capital needed a federal guarantee on up to $139 billion of its debt in 2008, GE was dropped from the Dow in 2018 as the last of the index's original members, and by 2024 it had split itself into three companies. In 2009 Welch himself called shareholder value "the dumbest idea in the world," which is a fair point and a strange one for him to make.

The theory outlived its author because GE was a factory for senior managers and executives, and its methods became standard business school teaching. Three of the five people who ran Boeing between 1996 and 2024 were Welch alumni. David Gelles traces this lineage in *The Man Who Broke Capitalism* (2022), and the Boeing half of this argument owes a lot to him.

Every tool in the kit works from what the accounting can see. RONA rewards owning less. The vitality curve treats people as interchangeable units of performance. Outsourcing assumes the work can be fully described in a contract. None of them has anywhere to record that the person leaving is the only one who knows why the health check is configured the way it is.

## Boeing, briefly

Harry Stonecipher, who had spent decades at GE, came to Boeing through its 1997 merger with McDonnell Douglas. He was plain about what he intended: to change the culture so that Boeing was "run like a business rather than a great engineering firm." In 2001 Boeing moved its headquarters 1,700 miles from its factories, and under Stonecipher and then Jim McNerney, another Welch alumnus, it outsourced most of the 787, sold its Wichita fuselage plant, put new engines on the aging 737 instead of designing a new airplane, and sent its cash to shareholders.

The bills arrived one airplane at a time.

The 787 came first. Handing most of it to suppliers was supposed to make it cheaper and faster to build, and it took far longer and cost far more than anyone at Boeing had imagined. It entered service more than three years late, and in 2011 the head of Boeing's commercial airplane business admitted, "We went too much with outsourcing."

The 777X, Boeing's next widebody, was launched in 2013 with first delivery planned for 2020. That delivery is now scheduled for 2027, and the program has cost Boeing about $15 billion in charges so far.

Then the 737 MAX. Two of them crashed catastrophically, killing 346 people, and in 2019 the worldwide fleet was grounded for nearly two years.

In January 2024 a panel blew out of an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 climbing out of Portland. It had been removed for rework at Boeing's factory and put back without its four bolts, and no record existed that it had ever been taken off, so nobody inspected it. The NTSB's final report is the clearest official portrait of an organization drained of its own engineering knowledge. Boeing's work instructions had been inadequate for years. The training, guidance and oversight its factory workers needed weren't there, and its safety management system was ineffective. The board's chair called it a breakdown in safety culture. What the report describes is a company that no longer reliably knew how to do a routine job on its own best-selling airplane.

## The rebuild cost

What Boeing gained by hollowing out its own engineering is easy to add up. Outsourcing the 787 and selling the Wichita fuselage plant were worth about $5 billion on paper, most of it development cost the suppliers were supposed to absorb. And from 2013 to 2019, Boeing spent about $43 billion buying back its own stock.

Undoing it has cost far more. The 787 ran to roughly $32 billion. Buying Spirit back cost $8.3 billion, nearly seven times what Boeing had sold it for. Boeing has lost more than $35 billion since the second MAX crash, its debt has quadrupled to about $54 billion, and in 2024 it sold $21 billion of new stock to shore up its finances, putting its share count back about where it was before the buybacks. Seven years on, it still can't build 737s at the rate it planned for 2019.

Shareholders didn't come out ahead either. Boeing's stock closed at about $440 in March 2019. In November 2026 it trades around $173, roughly 60 percent lower and about where it was a decade ago. The buybacks bought shares near the top. The people who did come out ahead were the executives paid on the earnings per share those buybacks propped up. Dennis Muilenburg, fired after the second crash, left with about $62 million in pension, deferred pay and stock awards.

## The rest of Big Tech

Boeing is still paying. The Big Tech version of the story begins in November 2022, when Elon Musk took Twitter private and cut about 80 percent of its staff, by his own count, and the site kept running. Meta announced 11,000 layoffs five days later. That first wave had its own causes, rising interest rates and pandemic over-hiring, but Twitter made deep cuts look survivable. Mark Zuckerberg said as much in June 2023, telling Lex Fridman that Musk's push to make Twitter leaner could "be good for the industry." Layoffs.fyi counted 122,606 tech layoffs in 2025 and 128,536 by September 2026, and those counts are a floor.

Musk plays the part in Big Tech that Welch played in American management a generation ago. Fortune named Welch its manager of the century in 1999, less for GE's results than because he had become "far and away the most influential manager of his generation." Executives who never worked for him ran his playbook anyway, and GE's stock price was all the proof they needed. Musk has the same standing among technology leaders now, and Twitter was his proof: cut four of every five people, tell the rest to commit to being "extremely hardcore" or leave, and the site stays up. Admirers in both cases looked at the first year and stopped looking.

What happened next at Twitter is, of course, telling. The site mostly stayed up, but less reliably: at least four widespread outages hit it in February 2023 alone, against nine in all of 2022, and when a fire at a data center near Portland took X down in May 2025, Musk conceded that its failover hadn't worked and that "significant improvements are needed across operations." The users drifted away. By early 2026, Similarweb put X's mobile daily users at about 126 million, down 12 percent in a year and behind Meta's Threads. The advertisers left faster: X's ad revenue for the first half of 2026 was $710 million, according to SpaceX's filings, against $4.5 billion for all of 2021. Fidelity, an investor in the original Twitter buyout, marked its stake down by nearly 80 percent.

Over-hiring plausibly explains some of the cuts of 2022. What explains the ones since is that Musk's playbook arrived just as the rest of the industry needed cash for something else. The AI data center build-out costs hundreds of billions of dollars a year, and every dollar of salary cut is a dollar that can go to chips and buildings without denting the margin. So the layoffs kept coming, even as these companies became some of the most profitable in history. Microsoft cut about 9,000 people in July 2025, one day into a new fiscal year, having earned $101.8 billion in the one that had just ended. Amazon cut 14,000 corporate jobs in October 2025 and 16,000 more in January 2026; its operating income for 2025 was $80 billion, a record. Meta cut about 8,000 in May 2026, after a year in which its operating income rose 20 percent to $83 billion. Before Welch, this would have read as a contradiction. A company earning $100 billion a year doesn't lay people off because it needs to. It does it because cutting is what a well-run company is supposed to do in a good year, and that idea is so thoroughly normal now that the earnings call and the layoff memo go out in the same month.

The rest of the kit has come back too. Cutting operating expense (salaries) to fund capital expense (data centers) is managing to the ratio in its 2020s form, and the memos say so directly: Meta's May 2026 memo said the cuts would "offset the other investments we're making," against more than $115 billion of capital spending, mostly on AI, and Andy Jassy has said Amazon's corporate workforce will shrink as AI makes it more efficient. Outsourcing now has a model as the supplier; Satya Nadella said in April 2025 that "maybe 20%, 30%" of the code in Microsoft's repositories was written by AI. And on October 26, 2026, Microsoft offered buyouts to about 60,000 employees, roughly a quarter of its workforce. The November outage began seventeen days later, before anyone had taken one.

Forced ranking is back as well, and it does the most lasting damage. Amazon and Meta both rank their staff against targets for pushing out about 5 percent of employees a year, set in advance and applied to every team however strong it is. Amazon calls its version unregretted attrition. If a team has no one who belongs at the bottom, someone ends up there anyway. A quota like that hits harder at a company that has stopped growing. When headcount is rising, the people pushed out are a small share of a workforce adding people around them. When headcount is frozen, every slot the quota opens is filled, if it's filled at all, by someone new to the systems. At 5 percent a year, that turns over nearly a quarter of a team in five years. Add two 10 percent layoffs along the way and more than a third of the original team is gone, and that doesn't account for the people who leave on their own. What walks out is a company's knowledge of how it builds and ships its own technology: how a change gets made safely, which process exists for a reason, who to call when something breaks in a way nobody has seen before. That knowledge is what lets a technology company execute well and move quickly. A quota records its loss as attrition nobody regrets.

Regardless, Big Tech has booked the savings. The cost of all this just hasn't fully arrived yet.

## Why the bill arrives late

If the cuts run this deep, why does everything still mostly work?

Because engineering capital isn't on the books. When an engineer leaves, the income statement records a saving and nothing else. Peter Naur, a well-regarded computer scientist, argued in a 1985 essay that a program is a theory held in the heads of the people who built it, and that the code is only a partial record of that theory. The finance department can see the salaries. It can't see the theory.

And because mature systems run on momentum. A company can lose the people who understand its systems and keep shipping for years, as long as nothing new is asked of those systems. The loss shows when something breaks in a way nobody has seen before, or when the company tries to build something new on top: a recovery that takes days, a launch that slips by a year.

The capital that matters most is the capital that shows least. Shared platforms and infrastructure have no customer of their own, so when headcount follows visible product work, they lose. Their failures are also the ones that take everything down at once.

The lag outlasts the people who cause it. The bill lands a decade or more after the decision, long after the executive who booked the savings has moved on. McNerney approved the 737 MAX in 2011. Dennis Muilenburg was running Boeing when the planes crashed.
## Buying it back

Boeing's rebuild came in a familiar order: first the regulators, then an engineer in the chief executive's chair, then the checks to buy back what it had sold. Big Tech is already at the first step. In July 2026 the UK put AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft and Oracle under the Bank of England's oversight, and every long outage makes the case for more.

The rest is coming. The engineers who could have told these companies what they were losing are, in the companies' own records, attrition nobody regretted. At Boeing, it took about twenty years to find out what that kind of accounting costs. Big Tech started the clock in 2022.
